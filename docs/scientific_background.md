# Scientific background

## Organoid biology and model boundaries

Organoids are three-dimensional cell systems that self-organize through lineage-specific differentiation and cell–cell interactions to reproduce selected structural or functional features of an organ. Adult stem cells can maintain tissue-restricted epithelial programs, while embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be guided through developmental signalling trajectories. Culture commonly combines a supportive extracellular matrix or defined hydrogel with timed growth-factor cues. The result is a model of selected tissue features, not a miniature complete organ.

A spheroid is principally a three-dimensional aggregate and need not reproduce organ-specific architecture. An assembloid intentionally combines separately patterned organoids or cell populations to study interactions between regions. An organ-on-chip adds engineered fluid flow, geometry, mechanics, or interfaces; it may host organoids but is not itself an organoid. These distinctions matter because implantation of a cell suspension, an epithelial monolayer, a spheroid, or a fetal tissue fragment cannot automatically be interpreted as organoid transplantation.

Organoid maturation is constrained by diffusion, incomplete stromal and immune compartments, non-physiological matrix, developmental immaturity, and batch variability. Transplantation can expose a graft to perfusion and systemic signals and may improve maturation, but xenogeneic animal environments introduce their own confounding biology.

## Stem-cell sources

Adult stem-cell organoids often preserve tissue-specific epithelial identity and can be expanded from a biopsy. Autologous use may reduce alloimmune mismatch, but diseased or genetically susceptible tissue can retain relevant defects, and many adult-stem-cell cultures omit mesenchymal, vascular, neural, and immune compartments.

ESCs provide pluripotency and established developmental protocols but create ethical and allogeneic-compatibility questions. iPSCs offer pluripotency with the possibility of patient-specific derivation, yet reprogramming, line-to-line variation, genomic stability, differentiation fidelity, time, and cost remain material concerns. Tissue progenitors occupy an intermediate position: they can be developmentally appropriate and responsive to organogenic cues but may have limited availability or expansion capacity.

## Retinal applications

Retinal organoids generate stratified neural retinal populations including photoreceptor precursors. Preclinical sheet transplantation can preserve spatial organization better than dissociated delivery and has produced maturation, host contact, and visual-response signals in degeneration models. These experiments remain distinct from RPE-cell therapy. The RPE is a supportive pigmented monolayer external to the neural retina; transplantation of ESC- or iPSC-derived RPE cells or patches is not transplantation of a complete retinal organoid.

The mapped retinal studies are animal experiments. They support graft survival, photoreceptor differentiation, and selected physiological responses, but do not establish durable human vision restoration. Surgical placement, rosette formation, immune management, off-target tissue, tumorigenicity, product identity, and reproducible manufacture remain central issues.

## Intestinal and colonic organoids

Adult LGR5-positive epithelial stem cells can form long-lived intestinal or colonic organoids. In mouse injury models, expanded colonic organoids have integrated into damaged mucosa and formed self-renewing epithelium. PSC-derived human intestinal organoids include epithelial and mesenchymal compartments and mature after ectopic implantation, developing crypt–villus features, brush-border enzymes, and host-derived vasculature.

Ectopic renal-capsule or mesenteric models are valuable for developmental and tissue-engineering studies, but they do not directly demonstrate luminal delivery, mucosal coverage, peristaltic integration, or function in diseased human bowel. In a colitis-model study in this corpus, injected iPSC-derived organoids were associated with earlier recovery signals but did not engraft in the injured colon. This is why engraftment and functional outcomes are coded separately.

## Kidney organoids

Directed differentiation of human PSCs can generate nephron-like segments containing podocyte, proximal-tubular, and distal-tubular populations. After implantation beneath the kidney capsule or at other vascular sites, host vessels can perfuse glomerular-like structures, basement membrane and podocyte architecture can mature, and tracer experiments can demonstrate size-selective movement and tubular uptake.

These are important functions at the microtissue level, not evidence of a replacement kidney. Kidney organoids typically lack a connected collecting system, ureteric outlet, organ-scale nephron mass, mature endocrine and homeostatic performance, and validated long-term safety. Endothelium may be largely host derived, while off-target and stromal populations can expand. Batch consistency and integration of nephron, collecting-duct, stromal, vascular, and urinary drainage systems remain major barriers.

## Liver organoids

Hepatic organoids and organ buds can be generated from adult progenitors or PSC-derived endoderm, sometimes with endothelial and mesenchymal partners. A landmark iPSC-derived liver-bud experiment showed rapid connection to host vessels, human protein production and drug metabolism, and rescue in a mouse liver-failure model. The graft was an auxiliary immature tissue and did not replace the recipient liver.

Translation requires durable hepatocyte maturation, biliary drainage, vascular organization, metabolic scale, avoidance of thrombosis or ectopic tissue, immune compatibility, and reproducible production. “Liver organoid” also covers biologically different epithelial-only, cholangiocyte, hepatocyte, and multi-lineage constructs, so evidence should not be pooled without qualification.

## Brain organoids

Brain and cortical organoids model aspects of neural development, regional patterning, disease-associated phenotypes, and network activity. Transplantation into rodent brain improves access to vasculature and physiological input. Included studies report survival, host vascularization, neuronal maturation, axonal projections, sensory responses, and circuit interaction.

These systems are primarily research models. They do not establish a clinically accepted graft treatment for neurological disease. Interpretation must address region identity, off-target cell types, incomplete microglial and oligodendroglial representation, variable organization, seizure or overgrowth risk, immune response, and long-term effects. Human neural tissue in animal brains also raises ethical questions about welfare, altered behaviour, and the degree of human-like neural function; oversight should be proportionate to the construct and experiment.

## Translational barriers

Incomplete vascularization limits oxygen and nutrient delivery and promotes necrosis; after implantation, host-derived vasculature may support survival but creates a chimeric system. Immaturity affects tissue-specific function. Off-target cells, genetic instability, and residual pluripotent cells raise tumorigenicity concerns. Immune rejection persists for allogeneic products, while autologous manufacturing may be slow and expensive. Graft–host integration must include not only survival but also correct architecture, innervation, vascular connection, drainage, mechanical compatibility, and physiological feedback.

Manufacturing requires defined raw materials, scalable culture, release criteria, potency assays, batch comparability, transport and preservation, and long-term surveillance. Matrigel and other animal-derived matrices are poorly suited to clinical manufacture. Regulatory evaluation must account for the combined features of cells, scaffolds, gene editing, surgical delivery, and potentially permanent living tissue. Ethical concerns include embryo-derived lines, consent for donor cells, genomic data, chimeric animals, and neural organoids.

## Evidence stages and human translation

In vitro characterization tests a construct outside a living recipient. Ex vivo experiments maintain isolated tissue or organs outside the body. Animal engraftment tests survival and integration in vivo but remains indirect evidence for humans. Human feasibility research may establish that a procedure can be delivered and monitored; it is not proof of benefit. Interventional clinical trials prospectively evaluate safety and outcomes under a protocol. Approved therapies have satisfied a jurisdiction-specific regulatory process for a defined indication.

The current 15-study evidence map contains only preclinical in vivo reports. Related human RPE-cell work and early intestinal feasibility activity do not change that classification because they are not eligible published organoid-graft outcome studies. No conclusion in this project should be read as clinical advice or as evidence that an organoid therapy is approved.
